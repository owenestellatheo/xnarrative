BOTTOM LINE
Tehran discourse is dominated by economic grievance, with the Pezeshkian administration getting cautious benefit-of-the-doubt on diplomacy but sustained criticism over inflation and the rial collapse. US-directed sentiment splits sharply along language lines — Farsi-language posts increasingly weary, Arabic-language posts more hostile.

Narrative

The dominant Farsi-language narrative centers on economic distress — rial volatility, food prices, and pension shortfalls account for roughly 41% of on-topic content. The Pezeshkian administration receives mixed-to-supportive sentiment on foreign policy posture (37% support / 24% oppose / 39% mixed) but predominantly negative sentiment on economic management. This is a notable divergence from typical Iranian political Twitter, where domestic and foreign policy sentiment usually move together.

Sentiment toward the United States is strongly negative across all three languages (62% oppose aggregated), but the texture differs: Farsi-language discourse frames US policy as a constraint on Iranian economic life, while Arabic-language posts (largely Gulf-origin) emphasize regional posture and the Houthi file. English-language posts skew toward diaspora commentary and human rights — a genuinely separate conversation that aggregation flattens.

Notable absence: very little discussion of the nuclear file relative to baseline. Either the topic is being deprioritized in real-time, or it's being routed to channels not captured by this search.

Cross-language divergence

The three language communities are talking about meaningfully different things. Farsi: lived economic experience and cautious diplomatic hope. Arabic: regional balance-of-power, mostly hostile framing toward both Tehran and Washington. English: diaspora-driven human rights and prisoner cases. An analyst should not treat aggregated sentiment as representative of any one audience.
